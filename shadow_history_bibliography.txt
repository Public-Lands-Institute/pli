RESOLVED 2026-09-07 — Camp Sherman influenza death toll. The field gives 1,177 dead by the end of October 1918, out of 5,686 cases reported at the camp that month, with 131 deaths on October 8, the worst single day.

That figure is the one with both a stated scope and a traceable source. The Ross County History Depot mortality schedule states: "In the month of October, 1918, 5,686 cases of the flu were reported there. By month's end, 1,177 had died," and credits "The Historical Report of Camp Sherman" and G. Richard Peck, The Rise and Fall of Camp Sherman. The same sheet prints the daily death count for October 1 to 13: 16, 22, 47, 52, 104, 119, 104, 131, 97, 86, 53, 49, 83, totalling 963 deaths in the first thirteen days.

The competing figure of 1,777 appears on the National Park Service page and in the Ohio History Connection blog. Neither states a period or cites a source, both attach it to the same 5,686 case count, and 1,777 would require roughly 814 further deaths across the back half of October, after the daily counts had already fallen from the October 8 peak of 131 to 83 by October 13. 1,777 and 1,177 differ by a digit transposition, and the daily schedule does not support the higher number. A third account giving 842 deaths out of 7,618 hospital admissions is measuring hospital admissions rather than reported cases and is not comparable.

The contemporary primary source (JAMA / War Medicine, "The Epidemic of Influenza at Camp Sherman, Ohio," February-March 1919) survives on PubMed Central only as page scans, not machine-readable text, and would settle the point definitively for anyone with library access.

GEOLOGY

1.47 Ga is Mesoproterozoic (Calymmian), not Rhyacian, which is 2300 to 2050 Ma. The St. Francois Mountains igneous rocks belong to the 1.48 to 1.45 Ga magmatism of the Eastern Granite-Rhyolite Province on the southern Laurentian margin, not to Midcontinent Rift precursor activity; the Midcontinent Rift is roughly 1.1 Ga. Corrected at Johnson's Shut-Ins State Park and Elephant Rocks State Park.
  https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/pp1866
  https://www.usgs.gov/publications/petrology-and-geochronology-148-145-ga-igneous-rocks-st-francois-mountains-terrane

The modern Ohio River at Cincinnati was established when pre-Illinoian ice dammed the west-flowing Teays River and formed glacial Lake Tight, roughly 730,000 to 900,000 years ago, not by Illinoian glaciation about 400,000 years ago. Corrected at Bender Mountain Nature Preserve.
  https://ohiodnr.gov/wps/wcm/connect/gov/ohio+content+english/odnr/discover-and-learn/safety-conservation/about-odnr/geologic-survey/glacial-geology/ice-age-in-ohio
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Tight
