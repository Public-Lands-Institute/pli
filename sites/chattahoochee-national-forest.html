Native lands Glossary →

Cherokee (Aniyvwiya); the Unicoi Turnpike, built 1814-1819 over a Cherokee foot trail used for millennia, crossed Unicoi Gap in this exact area (present GA Highway 75/17 through Helen), reducing travel between north Georgia and eastern Tennessee from months to weeks; in winter 1838-1839 the Unicoi Turnpike served as the first leg of the Trail of Tears as Cherokee people from northeast Georgia were marched at gunpoint toward Indian Territory.

Displacement & Tenure

Cession: Treaty of Hopewell (1785) began federal recognition of Cherokee boundaries, eroded by subsequent treaties through the early 1800s; gold discovered near Dahlonega in 1828, in the immediate vicinity of this forest, sparked the first major U.S. gold rush and an estimated 15,000-miner "Great Intrusion" onto Cherokee land, directly accelerating Georgia's push for removal; Cession: Treaty of New Echota (December 29, 1835), signed by the unauthorized Treaty Party faction (Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot) without the sanction of Principal Chief John Ross or the Cherokee National Council, ceded all remaining Cherokee territory in Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina, and Alabama for $5 million, ratified by the U.S. Senate in May 1836 by a single vote; enforced by federal troops in the 1838-1839 Trail of Tears removal. Georgia's 1832 land lottery distributed the ceded Cherokee territory, including the present forest area, to white settlers before removal was complete.

Shadow History

USGS and university stream-sediment studies have documented legacy mercury contamination in the Dahlonega Gold Belt from historic hydraulic and stamp-mill mining, with elevated concentrations still measurable near former mine sites within the forest's watersheds. CCC Camp Enotah (Company 431, SP-2), based at Vogel State Park, a state-park inholding within the forest boundary near Blairsville, built much of the forest's early recreation infrastructure in the 1930s, including campgrounds still in use today.

Ecology

Southern Appalachian cove hardwood and northern hardwood forest, with oak-hickory and mixed pine-hardwood on drier slopes; understory rich in mountain laurel, rhododendron, and flame azalea, with old-growth hemlock stands (many impacted by hemlock woolly adelgid) along stream corridors; supports black bear, white-tailed deer, wild turkey, and numerous salamander species characteristic of the southern Blue Ridge.

Hydrology

Headwaters of the Chattahoochee River (which supplies metro Atlanta) rise in this forest, alongside the Toccoa River and Chattooga River (Wild and Scenic) headwaters; Dukes Creek and Frogtown Creek (site of DeSoto Falls) are tributaries within the Chattahoochee and Chestatee watersheds respectively. Blue Ridge crystalline bedrock, no karst.
