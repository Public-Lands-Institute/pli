Ohoopee Dunes Wildlife Management Area

GA

A 9,271-acre Georgia wildlife management area of sandhill scrub, home of the endemic Georgia plume, on dunes whose longleaf pine was cut by sawmills and turpentine operators from the 1870s through the 1960s.

Late Pleistocene (Wisconsin Glaciation)

Native lands Glossary →

Yuchi (Tsoyaha, "Children of the Sun"), whose towns anchored the nearby Ogeechee River country before their westward displacement in the early 1700s under Creek and Carolina pressure; Lower Creek (Muscogee) towns then hunted, fished, and held Green Corn ceremonies along the Ohoopee, Ogeechee, and Canoochee rivers, leaving arrowheads and other artifacts across the dune fields that looters still disturb today

Displacement & Tenure

Cession: Treaty of Augusta (1773), the "New Purchase" extending Creek and Cherokee cession lines in Georgia to relieve traders' debts; Cession: Treaty of Augusta (November 1, 1783), in which Creek negotiators ceded the land between the Ogeechee and Oconee rivers, including the Ohoopee River dune country, over the strong public objection of the wider Creek Nation, which repudiated the sale as unauthorized. Georgia distributed the ceded land to settlers through its land lottery system beginning in 1805, and organized Emanuel County from Bulloch and Montgomery counties in 1812.

Shadow History

The dune country's longleaf pine uplands were consumed by the forest economy that was Emanuel County's mainstay from the arrival of the railroads in the 1870s through the 1960s: sawmills cut the virgin longleaf for timber while turpentine operators, many of them North Carolina naval stores producers who moved south with their equipment and Black labor forces into residential camps on large turpentine farms, bled the standing trees for rosin and spirits, an industry in which Georgia led the nation from 1890 to 1905 and again from the 1920s until the 1960s. The scattered longleaf and wiregrass the wildlife management area now holds with prescribed fire are remnants of that cutover landscape. Georgia ran a segregated park system through the same period, operating Yam Grande near Swainsboro in this county as a Black-only state park while the main system served white Georgians. Looting of Creek and earlier artifacts continues in the loose dune sand, and no prosecution for it at this site appears in accessible public records.

Ecology

Desert-like sandhill scrub built around Georgia plume (Elliottia racemosa), the state's only endemic tree, alongside stunted turkey oak, wiregrass, and aromatic shrubs adapted to the deep, fast-draining dune sand, with isolated longleaf pine and gopher tortoise burrows on the driest ridges. The dune system is the only place on Earth for the Ohoopee geometer moth (Fernaldella georgiana, tied to its host plant woody goldenrod), Donaldson's lined june beetle (Polyphylla donaldsoni), and the Ohoopee Dunes scarab beetle (Geopsammodius ohoopee), plus a grasshopper (Melanoplus stegocercus) found only here and at one outlying population at Yuchi WMA. Prescribed fire holds back woody encroachment.

Hydrology

The Ohoopee River, a tributary of the Altamaha River, and its tributary the Little Ohoopee River run along the dune fields' western edge; the Altamaha ultimately reaches the Atlantic near Darien.
